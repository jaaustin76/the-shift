# THE SHIFT — what the game is

> **Run a parcel distribution centre, and face what actually goes wrong in one.**

That sentence is the whole design. Everything below serves it, and anything that doesn't
belong to it doesn't go in.

---

## The promise

Not "optimise a warehouse." **This is what it's actually like.** The game's content is
*problems* — real ones, in the order and shape a real building produces them. Jams at
4am, a trailer that shows up late and wrecks the plan, someone quitting in week three,
peak season arriving whether you're ready or not.

That is also the moat. Anyone can build a conveyor game. This one is built by someone
who has stood in the building when the sorter died, and that shows in the details or the
whole thing is pointless.

**Tone:** grounded, dry, affectionate. Never cartoonish, never grim. The people are
competent adults doing a hard job, not comic relief.

## The spine

**Early game you operate. Late game you architect. Hiring carries you between them.**

- **Operate.** One line, a handful of chutes, three people. You move them yourself. You
  clear the jams yourself. You learn what a bottleneck *feels* like by standing in one.
- **Supervise.** More floor than you can personally attend. You hire people to take over
  the jobs you were doing, and drop into whichever aisle is failing.
- **Architect.** You stop touching the floor and start designing it. The problems you
  used to fix by hand, you now design out.

Every stage makes you want the thing the previous stage taught you to want.

## The rule that keeps you in the game

**Hired staff are always worse than you at the thing they replace.**

Your engineer reaches a jam eventually and sometimes fumbles it. Your floor manager sends
someone to the full chute a bit late, and picks the wrong someone. Delegation is never an
upgrade — it is a **capacity decision with a quality cost.** You hire because you cannot
be in two aisles at once, and you feel the difference every single time.

Without this rule the player optimises themselves out of their own game and the late game
becomes an idle game. This is not a balance knob. It is load-bearing.

## Growth

One building that grows — not a building you optimise forever, and not one you leave.
You start with one line, one bay door, one trailer. You end with a facility. Floor space,
dock doors, lines and equipment are all bought, and they compete with each other and with
hiring for the same credits.

**The floor plan is where the depth lives.** This is measured, not assumed: how you play
a shift swings throughput about 2%; what the building is made of swings it about 79%.
See `FINDINGS.md`. The shift is the *test* of your building, not the game itself.

## The shift never retires

There is always one aisle you can drop into. It becomes optional once you're good, but it
never goes away — it's how the numbers stay connected to something physical, and it's what
makes the architecture stage mean anything.

## The challenge catalogue

Content is problems. Each one is a real thing that goes wrong plus the system you need to
deal with it. This list is the roadmap's raw material and it extends indefinitely, because
the job keeps producing new ones.

**In the game now:**
- Chutes filling and backing the line up
- Jams
- No-reads at the scanner
- Trailer cutoff times and dock gaps
- Being understaffed
- Turnover

**Next, roughly in the order you'd meet them:**
- A late inbound trailer wrecking the morning plan
- Equipment breakdown — down, not just jammed
- Volume spikes nobody warned you about
- Mis-loads found after the trailer has gone
- Damaged freight and rework
- Oversize and non-conveyable that can't ride the line at all
- Peak season
- Safety incidents, and the pressure between speed and doing it right
- New hires being slow before they're useful
- Absenteeism — you planned for eight and six turned up

## Deliberately parked

Real ideas, not v1. They go in the roadmap as later kernels, not into the current build.

- **Intersections and contention** — two lines wanting the same square, yielding that
  propagates backward. Genuinely combinatorial, and a different source of depth than crew
  allocation (which is measurably shallow).
- **Multi-level belts** — bridges and underpasses as the expensive fix you buy for a
  choked crossing. Pairs with intersections: one is the problem, the other is the relief.
- **Ladder logic** — programming your own diverter rules instead of babysitting them.
  The natural endgame of the architect stage.

## Non-goals

- Not a twitch game. Reaction speed measurably doesn't matter and shouldn't.
- Not an idle game. If your hands stop mattering, a rule has been broken.
- Not a management spreadsheet. Every number has to be visible as a physical thing
  happening on a floor.
- Not cartoon warehouse. The details are real or the whole premise fails.
